What this weighting implies

Red line = equal split. This row is what the weighting implies: an input to a negotiation, not the answer.

Every section weighting lands near 28 / 25 / 28 / 19, not 25 / 25 / 25 / 25. FeedBurner still chose equal. Why?

  • The model opens a negotiation. The scores are negotiable too.
  • Equal is fast, and it signals trust on a team that already has history.
  • Whatever the split, vesting and the 83(b) matter more.

  • Removes all financing risk
  • Co-founders with less wealth need this more
  • Fair price for current traction
  • Integration risk: product loses independence

Take the C round

  • Upside: ride the S-curve
  • VC fund economics want the big outcome
  • Dilution raises the exit bar
  • Employees wait longer for liquidity

Rich vs. King, the day-one fixes, and what's next

Rich vs. King: the four ventures

The day-one fixes

Weigh idea, capital, opportunity cost, and future role. Set once, at founding.

Equity accrues with at-risk contributions over time. The split settles itself.

Teams that split equity equally, especially quickly, raised their first round at lower valuations. Hellmann & Wasserman trace most of that to differences between the teams: a fast, even split is a signal investors notice.

Founder vesting (4 years, 1-year cliff) and the 83(b) election. Sign them while the team still gets along.

Most mistakes on the left board have one fix: a contract signed on day one.

83(b) day-counter

The 83(b) election must be filed within 30 days of the grant. No extensions.

Takeaways

Team quality compounds across ventures. Protect it.

Equity splits are incentive design, not a fairness exercise.

Raise before you must. Sell from strength.

Exit decisions are portfolio decisions. Founders differ in wealth and risk.

Day-one contracts prevent year-three lawsuits.
